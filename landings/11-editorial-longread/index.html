Where strategies go after the offsite

Most companies agree a strategy once a year and check it once a year. In between, the market moves and nobody writes it down. Strapivo argues the fix is a standing session, a radar on the outside world and a numbered record of every decision.

Strapivo November 2026 7 minute read

The offsite ends on a Thursday afternoon. The leadership team has spent two days on it. There is a slide with an ambition, a dozen goals and a short list of priorities, and everyone in the room has agreed to it. On Monday the slide goes into a shared folder. In most companies, nobody reads it closely again until the next offsite.

Nobody decides to abandon the plan. It fails quietly. A competitor cuts prices in March. A regulator publishes a draft in May. A key supplier changes hands in July. Someone notices each event and someone raises it in a meeting. Nobody connects it to the plan.

By the time the annual review comes round, the plan describes a market that no longer exists. Changing course has become expensive, because the cheap moment to change it was months ago.

IThe news is not the problem

Executives do not lack information. They get more of it than they can read. What they lack is a link between the news and the goals they said must succeed. An article about interest rates is noise until someone asks which goal it touches and whether it changes the odds.

Without that question, every new report starts the same debate from the beginning. The loudest person in the room sets the frame, and the meeting ends with a set of slides that nobody opens again.

IITwo ways to get it wrong

Companies that try to stay current fail in one of two directions. The first is inertia. The plan stays fixed because reopening it feels like admitting a mistake. The second is thrash. Every headline triggers a meeting, every meeting adds a priority, and the team stops believing in any of them.

The workable middle is closer to steering a ship. The team makes small corrections early and changes course once in a while. One leadership team put the distinction to us this way. "We're not changing strategy every week, but we are revising our tactics."

IIIA routine with a fixed shape

Strapivo builds what it calls a Strategic Design System, one place where a company's strategy is kept and used. Its routine for keeping that strategy current is called Dynamic Strategy, and the structure is small on purpose. There is one ambition per portfolio. Under it sit seven to twelve success criteria, each with a light that is green, yellow or red today. Up to four of those criteria are flagged as focus areas. When a team tries to flag a fifth, the software asks: "You're diluting focus and creating noise. Do you really want to?"

For each focus area, a radar watches the outside world. Its search brief is the criterion, its objectives and its risks. Each signal it finds is a dated observation with a source, marked as primary or secondary. Vivo, Strapivo's AI, sorts the signals first and gives a one-line reason for each. A named person then marks each signal relevant, irrelevant or unsure. The signals that person dismisses teach Vivo what counts as noise for this company.

When a signal changes the picture, Vivo proposes a new position for a risk, drawn as a ghost on the radar with a one-line reason. A person confirms or rejects it. Strapivo sets a strict rule for the danger zone. A move into the top right needs at least one primary source, "so that one blog post can't raise an alarm." A risk that sits there for fourteen days without a decision goes onto the session lead's "Needs you" list.

IVThe hour

All of this feeds one session. By default it runs for sixty minutes, weekly, fortnightly or monthly. Twenty-four hours before it starts, Vivo drafts the agenda from what changed: new signals, risks that entered the top right, parked questions that are due back, and activities at risk or off track. Old business comes first. Every item carries a one-line reason. The session lead edits the draft, and the room approves it as its first act.

Each item follows the same order. The evidence goes up first. Then everyone records a first take, an individual position given before the discussion and revealed together, "without the loudest voice anchoring us." Only then does the conversation start. The team can ask Vivo what doing nothing would cost, to sketch scenarios, to argue the other side or to imagine a black swan.

The five options

1Hold
The criterion is marked as reviewed on that date.
2Adapt
Vivo drafts changes to the objectives and activities.
3Pivot
Vivo drafts new wording for the criterion. It takes effect only when someone with authority confirms it.
4Take advantage
The team adds detour activities and leaves the criterion as it is.
5Not yet
The team asks what is missing. It parks the question with a trigger that brings it back, or lets it go with a reason.

Every item ends in one of five options. The last, Not yet, is the one most meetings lack. A parked question needs at least one trigger to bring it back: new evidence, a named person's view, a date or the next session. Strapivo designed Not yet "so that it feels like parking, not losing."

VThe record

At the end of each item, Vivo reads back the decision for the Record of Decisions. The entry names anyone who disagreed and gives their reason. The session lead confirms it, and it gets a permanent number. Decisions beyond the team's authority go to an approver, as a memo or into the board pack.

Months later, a board member asks why the company held its prices in a falling market. One search finds the decision, the evidence behind it, the dissent and what happened next.

VIWhat the machine may not do

Leadership teams are right to be wary of software that writes strategy. Most of them want "help, not a co-author with the pen." Strapivo draws that line in specific places. Vivo proposes and people decide. It can ask "Light still green?" when two focus activities fall off track, but it never changes the light itself. A pivot it drafts stays a draft until someone with authority confirms it. Changes to what the company has accepted as true wait for an Owner. The company's phrase for this is "The human stays in the chair."

Vivo also works from the company's own material. It reasons from the business model, mapped on the Business Model Canvas, and from the strategic memory the team has approved. Every recommendation traces back to that memory. Strapivo runs its infrastructure in the EU and designs the product for GDPR.

Setup asks for four to five hours of the leadership team's time. Strapivo does the reading first: the workshop report, the board deck, a pasted email. Vivo proposes where each item belongs. A typical prompt reads "This reads like an activity, not an objective. Move it down?" An Owner accepts or declines it.

VIIThursday, again

Go back to that Thursday afternoon. A year on, the slide still sits in its folder. The difference is a Tuesday in May, at ten o'clock. There are four items on the agenda, and the regulator's draft is item three. Everyone in the room has already written down a first take. Before lunch, the decision has a number.
